A WINDOW INTO DEEP TIME

Explore the evidence.
Imagine the landscape.

Pangea Earth combines a scientific plate reconstruction with an interactive globe. The timeline lets you watch continental motion from 300 million years ago to 100 million years into the future. Drag the slider or press Play, choose a direction, and adjust the speed. The labeled age buttons open detailed snapshots.

What does ancient geography show?

The labeled snapshots use Scotese & Wright (2018) PALEOMAP PaleoDEMs: reconstructed estimates of ancient land, shallow seas, elevations, and ocean depths. Shorelines follow estimated sea level, rather than simply rotating today’s coastline. Colors and relief shading are rendered from the published elevations.

These are regional reconstructions. The displayed 0.2° grids were filtered and resampled from coarse data; that does not provide 22 km geological accuracy. Fine features, including Tasmania and connections around Australia, cannot be resolved precisely. This layer uses the PALEOMAP reference frame, while the drift animation uses Müller et al. (2022), so positions can differ when switching views.

How does playback work?

Playback rotates simplified continental pieces using the same model’s finite rotations, sampled every 5 million years and smoothly interpolated between samples. It shows rigid continental motion, not a full simulation of oceanic crust creation, subduction, or continental deformation. Pieces appear or disappear according to their model validity ranges. Playback uses an overview surface; select a labeled snapshot to return to detailed zoom.

How is the future projected?

Future mode continues each plate’s average angular motion from the most recent 0–5 Ma model interval at a constant rate, up to 100 million years ahead. It keeps continental pieces valid today and extrapolates their rigid rotation. This is an illustrative what-if experiment, not a published future reconstruction or a prediction. It does not model changing plate boundaries, collisions, new subduction, sea level, deformation, or future terrain. Pieces can overlap as they converge.

For context, NASA describes several possible future supercontinent scenarios. This explorer does not reproduce those scenarios.

What is illustrative?

The untextured drift view uses artistic surface shading on rotated modern continental outlines. It does not show ancient shorelines or vegetation. The ancient geography snapshots use estimated paleo-elevations for color and shading, but the 3D globe itself is smooth. Zooming cannot reveal detail absent from either dataset.

Can satellite imagery move with the continents?

Enable Project modern satellite imagery to attach NASA’s modern Blue Marble imagery to each continental piece. The texture follows its model rotation smoothly. It is a visual projection of modern terrain and coastlines, not evidence of ancient vegetation, landforms, or shorelines.

Can I see satellite imagery?

Choose Satellite today to see NASA’s Blue Marble imagery of modern Earth. These satellite-derived composites have a native resolution of about 500 meters. They cannot show Pangea: satellites did not exist 240 million years ago.

Controls

Drag or swipe to rotate. Scroll or pinch to zoom. Right-drag to tilt. Use + / − for accessible zoom, N to face north, and ◎ to return to the whole planet.
